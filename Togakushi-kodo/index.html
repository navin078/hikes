Walking the Togakushi Kodo

From Ichino-torii to Okusha: the practical guide I wish I had before setting out.

Why I put this guide together

Before walking the Togakushi Kodo, I spent quite some time looking for a practical guide to the route from Ichino-torii to Okusha. I found maps, descriptions of the shrines, and photographs of the famous cedar avenue. What I struggled to find was an account of the actual walk: where the trail begins, which turns to take, how long the sections take, and what to expect along the way.

So, having walked it myself, I thought I'd put together the guide I wish I'd had. This is a sacred mountain landscape and a pilgrimage linking five shrines, not just a hike. My account is based on October 28, 2026, including some wrong turns. I've tried to be accurate, but paths and conditions change. Use this alongside current local information.

Should you walk the whole route?

I walked from Ichino-torii Garden to Okusha, visiting all five shrines. I'm glad I did it once. But for a first visit focused on the shrines and cedar avenue, I'd start at the bottom of the Hokosha stairs and use the saved time and energy for Kagami-ike (Mirror Pond), if accessible.

For most visitors: start at Hokosha, visit Hinomikosha and Chūsha, then continue to the Okusha entrance and through the cedar avenue to Okusha and Kuzuryusha. Start at Ichino-torii if you specifically want the longer pilgrimage, especially its rubbing stones.

The route at a glance

  1. Ichino-torii Garden parking area: arriving from Nagano, walk to the far, uphill end of the parking lot. Look for the arrow sign that actually says “Ichino-torii” in English. I lost time taking more obvious-looking uphill paths instead. The correctly signed path initially runs through a beautiful sunken, mossy cutting for about ten minutes, then largely hugs the main road, with occasional wooded sections, toward Hokosha.
  2. Hokosha: climb the steep stone staircase with its central handrail. This is also my recommended starting point for the shorter walk. From the shrine, the onward trail is to the right.
  3. Hinomikosha: follow the signed branch between Hokosha and Chūsha. I initially missed it and had to backtrack about 150 metres to visit the shrine.
  4. Chūsha: continue to the left of the shrine as you face it, past the goshuin desk and parking area. Follow the signs, crossing the road as needed. The onward path has dirt and log steps; farther along, the route briefly rejoins the road before crossing back to the trail. Allow roughly half an hour from Chūsha to the Okusha approach entrance.
  5. Okusha entrance: the entrance to the wide walking path from the parking area. Continue to the red Zuishinmon gate, through the ancient cedar avenue, and up the narrower, potentially muddy final approach to Okusha and Kuzuryusha.
  6. Return: descend toward Chūsha for food and the Nagano-bound bus. Kagami-ike is an optional separate detour only if the trail is open and you have time and energy.

The junction I got wrong

Just before Hokosha, the trail reaches the main road. A tempting forest road branches away, and I followed it for roughly twenty minutes before realising my mistake. Instead, look across the main road for a tall wooden pilgrimage sign beside a black rubbing stone high on a raised roadside berm. Stay with the main road toward Hokosha rather than taking the attractive forest road. Read the arrows carefully: the sign names several destinations, and the direction toward Chūsha and Okusha is not the direction you want if you haven't visited Hokosha yet.

Navigation warning: the most inviting path isn't necessarily the pilgrimage route. Pause at the road, find the sign and check your direction before committing.

Five shrines, one day

The five shrines make a satisfying journey in their own right. Hokosha’s stairs are steep; Hinomikosha is a short detour; Chūsha sits at the heart of the route. Beyond the Okusha entrance, Zuishinmon and the ancient cedar avenue lead toward Okusha and Kuzuryusha. The final climb was muddy and snowy on my visit.

Five goshuin, three desks

I wanted all five shrine goshuin together, so I began a new goshuincho at Hokosha. My previous book still had two pages left, but I didn't want to split the five across books. The new book, available at Togakushi, has a colourful illustration of Hokosha's stairs and torii, mountains, trees and wildflowers on its cover.

Hokosha: ask for both Hokosha and Hinomikosha at the desk. Hinomikosha was unattended, and I needed to request its goshuin separately. Chūsha: one goshuin at its own desk. Okusha: the same counter handled both Okusha and Kuzuryusha; ask for both.

When the monk at Okusha noticed I had collected all five, he gave me a commemorative bookmark marked 五社参拝 (visiting all five shrines). I don't know whether every visitor who completes the five receives one, so treat this as my experience rather than a promise.

The rubbing stones: a pilgrimage within the pilgrimage

The Togakushi Kodo has 30 rubbing stations along the historical route. I spotted only 21. Perhaps I wasn't observant enough; I also suspect I sometimes followed a parallel walking path rather than the old pilgrimage trail. Reaching all five shrines doesn't necessarily mean passing every rubbing stone.

The evening before the hike, I asked at the Nagano Station Tourist Information Center about a rubbing kit. They did not sell one. The helpful staff called Togakushi and learned that the kit was available at the Togakushi Tourist Information Center at Chūsha. But Chūsha comes after many of the stones if you're starting at Ichino-torii. If collecting rubbings is your goal, obtain the kit before starting. Check availability directly with the Togakushi centre.

Some stations are hard to access. One stone I found sat so high on a roadside berm that making a rubbing would have required scrambling up to it. I photographed it instead. Don't risk a fall for an impression.

Things I'd tell the next walker

Getting there

I prebooked a taxi through my Nagano hotel to Ichino-torii Garden: approximately 9.9 km, about 20 minutes, ¥4,000 that morning. The driver asked repeatedly whether I was sure about the drop-off. Don't assume a bus stops at that parking area.

Food and water

Carry lunch, water and snacks. I had an egg sandwich in the morning, a vending-machine latte and emergency cookies, but no substantial meal until carbonara at about 1:15 PM. I was exhausted and nauseated by the descent.

Weather and footing

Cold rain became snow near Okusha. Wet layers and overheating followed by stopping made temperature management difficult. Keep an insulating layer dry, and wear shoes with grip: the final climb was muddy.

Restrooms and respect

Togakushi is a sacred mountain. Plan restroom stops rather than treating the forest as a toilet. I saw facilities around Chūsha, near the Okusha approach entrance/parking area, and beyond Zuishinmon gate before the final climb. These are my observations, not an exhaustive list. The official map marks WCs.

Food stops aren't guaranteed

The café at the Okusha entrance was closed on the Wednesday I visited, and two other restaurants had long waits. Kotorinomori, on the way back toward Chūsha, was my eventual refuge. Check opening days.

Mirror Pond

The Kagami-ike trail branch I encountered was closed for maintenance that day. Check access before planning a detour; don't assume my October 2026 closure still applies.

What I'd do next time

For the best shrine-and-forest day

I'd start at the foot of the Hokosha stairs, visit all five shrines, enjoy the cedar avenue, and spend the time saved at Kagami-ike if conditions allow.

For the rubbing-stone quest

I'd return to Ichino-torii with a kit already in hand and make following the old pilgrimage route and finding the stones the point of the day. That is a different, slower outing; I wouldn't try to squeeze both plans into one relaxed day.

The morning: the route nobody explained

The taxi driver asked three times whether I really wanted to be dropped at Ichino-torii. The parking lot map was confusing, and I tried the wrong uphill paths before spotting the trailhead actually marked Ichino-torii. For ten glorious minutes, the path ran through a deep, mossy cutting. Then it emerged near the road. I had imagined a day of secluded forest; the first half of the pilgrimage turned out to involve more roadside walking than expected.

At Nagano Station the evening before, the tourist information staff had kindly telephoned ahead about the rubbing kit. They discovered it was sold only at Chūsha, halfway through the route. I ended up photographing the stones instead.

The shrines and the cedars

The Hokosha stairs looked formidable, but the climb felt good. Hinomikosha required a short detour. Beyond Chūsha the trail improved, and the approach to Okusha was the part I'd been waiting for: a wide path, the red Zuishinmon gate, and then enormous cedars whose scale photographs struggle to convey. The last section was muddy from rain and snow. Okusha itself felt quiet and present against the forest and whitening mountainside.

The descent, the café, and the bus

By the descent I was cold, hungry and nauseated. The Kagami-ike branch was closed, the nearby café was shut, and other restaurants had long waits. A few cookies kept me going until Kotorinomori, where carbonara, coffee and a seat beside the heater were wonderful. At the Togakushi tourist information centre at Chūsha, I waited for the bus in the warmth and found free eki stamps and a free rubbing activity using an ordinary engraving, not a pilgrimage stone. After the previous evening’s hunt for a kit, the irony was hard to miss.

The 2:37 bus took a different road from my morning taxi. After a day of nature, the ride was all engineering: mountain roads looping across and underneath themselves, and a remarkable multi-level bridge. I was back at the hotel around 3:30, sore all over, and tried to sleep by seven.

Useful links

Getting back to Nagano by bus: Tourists should book the Alpico Nagano–Togakushi sightseeing bus in advance. Bus 70 is a separate, non-reserved service intended primarily for local residents. Treat the sightseeing bus as reservation-only in practice: I saw the driver turn away passengers who had not booked, explicitly telling them it was “reserve only.” Reserve your seat online before travelling; do not rely on buying a ticket from the driver. Suica and other transport IC cards are not accepted. Although Alpico’s published information mentions possible boarding without a reservation when seats remain, that was not what I observed on my trip. Check the current timetable and booking rules before travelling.

  • Kotorinomori café: where I finally got a hot meal on the return.
  • Loop bridge on the Nagano-bound bus route: a memorable piece of engineering after a day in the forest.
